How Seven Faith Traditions Talk About Peace

A source-labeled comparison of how Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, Sikhism and Taoism approach peace, non-hostility, compassion and right action.

Published Nov 2, 2026Source-labeled guide

Peace is not one identical teaching

Across religious traditions, words translated as peace, compassion, mercy, non-hostility or harmony can sound similar while belonging to very different theological and philosophical systems. Comparing them is useful only if the differences remain visible.

Christianity: peacemaking as active moral work

Matthew 5:9 praises peacemakers in the Beatitudes. The emphasis is not merely on feeling calm; peacemaking is presented as an active moral quality within Jesus’ teaching.

Buddhism: ending hostility without more hostility

Dhammapada 5 says that hatred is not ended by hatred. In its Buddhist setting, peace is connected with the transformation of intention, conduct and the mind.

Judaism: seek peace and pursue it

Psalm 34:15 links turning away from evil, doing good and actively pursuing peace. The verse presents peace as something to be sought rather than passively awaited.

Hinduism: compassion and absence of ill-will

Bhagavad Gita 12:13 describes a person who bears no ill-will toward any being and is friendly and compassionate. The verse belongs to a larger description of devotion and character.

Sikhism: without fear and without enmity

The Mool Mantar describes the Divine as without fear and without enmity. That language is foundational in Sikh scripture and should be understood within Sikh theology rather than reduced to a generic quote about kindness.

Taoism: harmony through non-contention

Tao Te Ching 8 compares the highest goodness with water, which benefits things without competing. The image connects humility, non-contention and natural order.

Islam: recognition across difference

Qur’an 49:13 describes peoples and tribes being made so that they may know one another. It is not simply a “peace verse,” but it is relevant to questions of human difference, recognition and moral status.

What comparison is actually good for

The value is not finding one universal sentence shared by every religion. It is seeing how different traditions frame moral life, community, hostility, compassion and harmony in their own language and context.
